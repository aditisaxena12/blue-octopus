*On Ali Sethi's song of longing, rooftops and a fish waiting to be caught by a crane*

I didn't understand a single line the first time I heard it, and it still broke my heart a little.

It was my BTech years in Goa (yes yes, it was like a dream, with classes on the beach and a beer in one hand. Just being sarcastic. I barely went to the beach like four times). A friend had this track playing on repeat, and before I knew it, it was stuck in my head. My grasp on Punjabi was nowhere near good enough to follow the words, but the sadness and longing came through anyway.

Soon it became *the* song for slow evenings, the winding-down catch-ups, and those stretches where you sit silently beside your friends, saying nothing, just staring at the stars. But it is true that whether you're exuberant, blue or just plain exhausted, Ali Sethi's baritone always finds a way to tug at something in your chest.

It took me years to ask what he was actually singing. The answer was even more beautiful than the mystery itself.

"Chan Kithan" literally translates to "Where was the moon?" It is a classical Punjabi-Sufi folk poem that has had multiple renditions over almost a century. The song is in the Saraiki dialect, which is spoken mainly in southern Punjab and northern Sindh in Pakistan, and its unmistakable soft, melancholic phonetics make it a favourite for Sufi poetry.

Punjabi folk forms (*tappay, mahiyey, dohrey*) also follow a particular structure: every alternate line exists to set up the rhythm for the line that follows, and that second line carries the meaning, which we see in this song as well. At its core, the song blends everyday rural domesticity with an intense existential pining, artistically using the moon as a symbol for an absent beloved.

In particular, Sethi's arrangement combines a traditional folk ditty from rural Punjab with electronica and indie-rock, creating what he describes as "Punjabi Gothic music." The video retells the Cinderella story in contemporary Pakistan.

The song opens with a direct address to the moon…

> *Chan kithan guzaari aayi raat ve? Menda jee daleelan de vaat ve*
>
> **In English:** My moon (beloved), where did you spend the night? My heart has been trapped in endless cycles of reasoning.

Here, the word *daleelan* means arguments, rationalizations or overthinking. Older traditional versions focus purely on the sorrow of waiting, but Sethi's delivery goes one step ahead and draws emphasis to the *psychological anxiety* of romantic alienation. The protagonist isn't just weeping on a roof, their mind is also spinning arguments, wondering whether the lover has strayed, forgotten them or met with danger.

The second verse moves on to the situation of the lover…

> *Kothre utte kothra maahi, kothe sukh diya toriyaan,*  
> *Kalliyan raatan jaag ke asaan giniyaan teriyaan dooriya ve*
>
> **In English:** Terrace upon terrace, my love, and gourds dry on the roof… / I have spent these lonely nights wide awake, counting the distance between us.

Here the song roots itself in traditional rural life. In villages, vegetables like *toriyaan* (gourds) are sliced and laid out on flat mud roofs to dry for the winter. The *kotha* (rooftop terrace) also becomes a haunting image of the physical divide: looking out across layer upon layer of rooftops, you sense the vast, empty space between the two lovers.

And finally the third verse brings in the hope of a meeting…

> *Kothre utte kothra maahi, kothe baitha kaan bhala,*  
> *Main bann javaan maachli, tu bagula ban ke aa bhalaan ve*
>
> **In English:** A crow sits on the roof… / Oh, let me transform into a fish, if only you would come swooping down as a crane to claim me.

The crow (*kaan*) is a traditional South Asian symbol of an impending arrival or message. The most intense imagery for me, though, is the shape-shifting motif of the fish (*machli*) and the white crane (*bagla*). In nature, a crane catching a fish means death for the fish. In Sufi poetry, it represents the ultimate surrender of the ego: the speaker longs for union so desperately that they are willing to be consumed entirely by the beloved. It is a plea for the ultimate connection even if that connection destroys them.

I may be extrapolating, but I feel that while capturing the raw ache of human romance, the song also explores deep spiritual currents, highlighting a lost soul's profound yearning to discover and embrace a faith to anchor itself to.

I also looked up the original traditional version of the song, which has one more verse that I especially loved:

> *Kothre utte kothra maahi, kothe sukhda gha bhala,*  
> *Ashiqan jodiyaan paudiyan, te mashuqa jode raah bhalaan ve*
>
> **In English:** My love, the grass is drying up on the roof… The lovers painstakingly built the steps (ladders), but the beloved has simply paved their own pathways away.

In an agrarian village, grass (*gha*) is meant to be green, fresh and fed to cattle. Leaving it on a mud roof to dry out, turn brown and wither implies utter neglect. The poet uses the landscape to describe their own state: *"Just like this forgotten grass on the roof, I am slowly wasting away, losing my youth and vitality while waiting for you."*

The second line is the one that gets me. To reach someone across the high, divided rooftops (*kothe*), a lover must build steps or ladders (*paudiyan*). That image captures the effort, vulnerability and risk the lover (*ashiq*) takes on to close the distance. The beloved (*mashuq*), however, does not climb up to meet them. Instead, they "pave a completely different road" (*jode raah*) and walk away into the distance. It reflects a universal truth of unrequited Sufi romance: the lover spends a lifetime building bridges, while the beloved remains elusive, independent and ultimately indifferent.

I think this is a good point for me to stop with the TMI and let you enjoy and embrace the song for yourself. Whether you listen to it while staring at the stars on a silent night or watching life go by on a crowded local train, this song will transport you to a world of your own, where you find yourself alone, with raw emotions, chasing the fading echo of a love never promised.
